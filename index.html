Interactive cartography

A series of browser-based exploratory spatial data analysis tools, built with D3.js. Each takes the linked-view brushing model from GeoDa — select features in one view and watch them highlight in the others — and makes it work on the open web with no install and no build step. Each is data-agnostic: point the config file at your own data and the tool adapts.

Choropleth EDA — Univariate

A classified choropleth linked to a histogram of the mapped variable. Lasso or rectangle-select features on the map to see where they fall in the distribution, or brush a range in the histogram to see where those features are. Quantile and equal-interval classification, 3–8 classes, 27 ColorBrewer palettes, and a summary statistics panel comparing the selection to the full population.

Live demo Source Demo data: NYC census tracts, 2020

Choropleth EDA — Bivariate

A 3×3 bivariate choropleth linked to a scatterplot with OLS regression. Selecting a subset draws a second regression line for that subset, so you can compare its slope and correlation against the full population directly. Six bivariate color schemes adapted from Joshua Stevens, with a floating color-matrix legend showing the class breaks.

Live demo Source Demo data: NYC COVID-19 vaccination rates by MODZCTA, 2021

Choropleth EDA — Two Maps

Two stacked choropleths with independent variables, palettes, and legends, sharing synchronized zoom and pan, plus a scatterplot of one against the other. Selection in any of the three views propagates to the other two. The layout divider is draggable, so the balance between map and chart is yours to set.

Live demo Source Demo data: NYC COVID-19 vaccination rates by MODZCTA, 2021

Pictogram Bivariate EDA — US States

The bivariate tool above, with the scatterplot's dots replaced by the states themselves. Each mark is the state's own outline, normalized so every silhouette encloses the same area and anchored on its area-weighted centroid — so a reader can pick Texas or Michigan out of the scatter by shape alone, without a tooltip or a legend lookup. Map and marks are cut from one pre-projected topology, which keeps a state's silhouette geometrically identical to its shape on the map. The trick only works for geographies whose outlines a reader already carries in their head, which is a real constraint and part of the point.

Live demo Source Data: presidential vote share, 1976–2024

Distribution charts

Unit charts, where one mark is one thing rather than an aggregate — the reader can count the population instead of trusting a bar. Both are built from generator scripts that fetch their sources at build time, so the tables can be rebuilt from scratch by anyone.

Presidential Ages in Office

A Wilkinson dot plot of how old presidents actually were while holding the office. One dot is one president at one integer age, and each dot is filled to the share of that year genuinely served — so William Henry Harrison's whole presidency is a single sliver at 68, and nobody is dropped by sampling. Colored by party, with the distribution running from Theodore Roosevelt at 42 to Joe Biden at 82.

Live demo Source Data: congress-legislators and Wikidata

Senate Ages by Congress

The age of every sitting senator on the day each Congress convened, across all 119 Congresses from 1789 to 2025 — 9,299 seats, median age 55. Not the incoming class but the whole chamber, so a senator serving thirty years contributes fifteen observations at fifteen different ages. Drag the timeline beneath the chart to brush an era and compare its distribution against the full series.

Live demo Source Data: congress-legislators

Network visualization

Applying the same D3 toolkit to non-spatial relational data.

Guardian Top 100 Novels — Vote Network

A force-directed network of the Guardian's 2026 best-novels poll, with a node for every novel and every voter and an edge for every vote cast. Edge length is weighted by ballot position, so critics with orthodox taste pull toward the center while idiosyncratic ballots and niche novels drift to the edges — the layout itself becomes the argument. Dashed edges connect novels that appeared together on eight or more ballots, surfacing the clusters critics tend to group. Filter by author, novel, or voter; labels fade in and out by zoom level and vote count so the graph stays readable at any scale.

Live demo Source Data compiled by Matthew Aldridge
